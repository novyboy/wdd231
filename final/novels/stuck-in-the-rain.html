“Stuck in the Rain”

by Nova Sonomura

        I held out a hand, hoping that’d somehow make the rain any less there. All it did was make my hand wet. I sighed. Y’know, I get that it usually rains in these kinds of stories—it’s romantic—whatever—I get that, but I don’t think it’s normally a typhoon, right?

        I flopped onto my friend coming up behind me, groaning away my strength until I was as limp and cumbersome as possible.

        “Riiiiikkaaaaaaa,” I mourned. My umbrella, which, for the record, I totally brought, was missing, and I’d spent the last five minutes speedrunning through the stages of grief. I crafted a haiku in my head:

        Oh school, you rascal

        Oh, you and your funny way

        Of not canceling ’til fifth period

        Five syllables too many, but I thought it had spirit.

        Rika placed me firmly upright, gripping my shoulders.

        “I’m sorry,” she began, sounding profound yet sickeningly melodramatic, “this umbrella’s not big enough for the both of us.”

        “Don’t try to sound cool while abandoning me.”

        She chuckled softly. Then, as her gentle laugh transformed into the guffaw of an absolute villain, she took off, wading into the watery depths beyond us.

        She made it give or take 20 feet before her umbrella gave out, and she was forced to retreat back to the safety of the foyer. Torrents of rain crashed down, leaving streaks suspended in their wake like scan lines on a TV. Watery globules rapped loudly on the rusty gutter overhead, creating illusory sounds of thunder in time with distant flashes, as if some foley artist were trying to convince us of their authenticity.

        Rika really didn’t need the convincing—she was drenched and looked as though she’d come up out of the ocean. Seeing her, all cold and pathetic—I was a bit glad she’d ditched me there.

        “Yeah…” she wrenched her skirt, liquid splashing down into a puddle at her feet, “that didn’t work.” Some ricocheted onto my leg, reminding me that I really should’ve worn pants.

        The storm made it hard to talk, so I messaged my mom. Unfortunately, she’d gotten stuck in traffic coming home from work, and while my dad was home (his contractor had postponed his construction work to prevent extra expenses), we only had one car.

        We stood for a while, gazing out awkwardly at the aquarium boundary imprisoning us there. After a long moment of silence (which is a bad way to describe it, given how incredibly noisy it was), she sneezed. It was quite unrestrained and horrible-sounding, but it let me know our ears had adjusted to the downpour.

        “Hey, d’you think your mom could drive us home?” I asked once she’d finished wiping her nose.

        “Ah… yeah, should be fine—I’ll check real quick…” she fiddled with her phone, struggling through moisture on the touchscreen, “Mmmm…”

        “No?”

        “No—she can,” she started unsurely, “buuut she’s driving back from the doctor’s right now, and she wants to fill up some water bottles in case the weather takes a turn for the worse.”

        “How long?”

        “Uhhh… I think…” She sucked through her teeth. “No, I don’t think—I’ll just ask.”

        Idly, I kicked at a pebble—regretted it immediately, though—it left a short tally mark scuff on my shoe. A notification filled the air—I peered over her shoulder, dampening my shirt.

        “What did she say?”

        “She guesstimates it’ll take around…” she lulled a second, as if she were the one coming up with the answer, “45-ish minutes?”

        I checked the time: 1:33… My mom guesstimated 2:40, so… math… uh…

        “Okay, she’s never been wrong before—I’ll tell my mom to go straight home, then.”

        The instant I sent the text, a strong gust of wind swept rain and everything in it hurdling almost horizontally, right onto us. So much for staying dry.

        Taking off our shoes (they were absolutely filthy now, and we weren’t about to get chewed out for making a mess of the hall), we fled indoors, our bare footsteps clumsy and loud in the empty corridor. Raindrop-beaten windows left dim impressions on the walls opposite to them. The sky was overcast; gloomy clouds soaked everything in a dull gray. Despite the droning at the entrance, the rain seemed peaceful here—a soothing tapping sound on the glass. I studied my surroundings—the sliding doors and classroom windows you wouldn’t see anywhere else. We didn’t live in Japan, but enough of Japan lived there that we may as well’ve.

        Rika shivered. “Ughhhh, I feel so grosssss.”

        I made a resigned affirmation; I was disproportionately more wet up front, so I was stuck doing that thing where you pull your shirt away from your skin—it never works, but everyone does it anyways.

        “Super gross.”

        She dug through her bag, searching for something.

        “EuGH—“ she held up a soggy sweater at arm’s length, “this is wetter than what I’m wearing.” She’d had her backpack on the wrong way ‘round, so it’d received the brunt of the damage.

        Out of pity, I checked if I had anything. “Oh,” I retrieved a navy blue raincoat from my bag.

        She looked at me judgmentally. "Why weren't you wearing that earlier?"

        "Look, do you want it or not?"

        "I want it, I want it."

        I tossed it over, and she quickly stripped off her clothes to change into it. It was a bit big for her, but it wasn't absolutely filthy, so she had no right to complain.

        "...I feel like an exhibitionist."

        "Hey."

        "Yeah, yeah—thank you very much." She slid the strap of her bag back over her shoulder.

        After making a few more rounds, but not wanting to explore the sketchier parts of the building, we returned to the entryway, hoping something had changed. It hadn’t. Like—at all. I took out my phone to check the time: 1:15. Gosh, we’re gonna be here forever, aren’t we? I lowered the screen and stuck my hand out into the rain.

(1000 words)
